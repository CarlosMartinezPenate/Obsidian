
# Introduction

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy, driving primary production and sustaining life on Earth (Field et al., 1998). This fundamental process begins with the absorption of light, whose intensity and spectral composition vary across habitats and over time.

Photosynthetic organisms capture light through specialized antenna systems, where proteins organize pigments that absorb photons and transfer excitation energy to reaction centers, which initiate its conversion into chemical energy (Zhao et al., 2024). Light-harvesting antenna complexes (LHC) support photosynthesis across diverse environments—from dim aquatic habitats and sun-exposed deserts to the interior of host cells and the familiar vegetation of gardens and backyards (Hoppe et al., 2024; Harel et al., 2004; Xiang et al., 2013). Their molecular organization, coupled with mechanisms that regulate their function, abundance, and turnover, enables organisms to capture available light while managing excess excitation (Iglesias-Prieto and Trench, 1997; Li et al., 2000; Hu et al., 2020). Across photosynthetic lineages, antenna systems differ in pigment composition, protein architecture, and assembly organization, reflecting distinct evolutionary histories and ecological contexts (Liu et al., 2004; Zhao et al., 2024). Understanding how these pigment–protein systems assemble and are regulated provides a molecular basis for explaining their light-harvesting properties and their responses to changing cellular requirements. This research proposal examines the molecular organization and regulation of two distinct photosynthetic antenna systems: in Dinophyceae, we will determine how composition and assembly relate to the spectroscopic properties of LHCs; in Cyanophyceae, we will examine how regulatory proteins affect LHC organization and disassembly. Together, we address how antenna components form functional molecular assemblies and how regulatory proteins alter those assemblies.

Symbiodiniaceae provide a system for investigating how distinct apoprotein–pigment scaffolds organize photosynthetic pigments. Their antenna machinery includes water-soluble peridinin–chlorophyll _a_ proteins (PCP) and membrane-associated chlorophyll _a_–chlorophyll $c_2$–peridinin complexes (acpPC) (Jiang et al., 2012, 2014). These antenna systems incorporate overlapping sets of pigments within different molecular architectures, offering a framework to examine how protein composition and assembly influence light-harvesting properties. Previous biochemical studies have identified functional trimeric acpPC assemblies (Jiang et al., 2014), while cryo-electron microscopy has resolved a _Symbiodinium_ photosystem I supercomplex surrounded by an organized array of acpPC antenna proteins (Zhao et al., 2024). These findings provide critical reference points for investigating the complexes recovered from the strains examined here. Chapter 1 will investigate which antenna assemblies can be reproducibly isolated from selected Symbiodiniaceae and how their molecular organization relates to their absorption and fluorescence properties. Tracking soluble and membrane fractions separately will support the identification of distinct antenna populations, with structural investigations pursued concurrently.

Understanding antenna regulation also requires determining how established pigment–protein assemblies are dismantled (Levi et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020). Chapter 2 will investigate these dismantling processes using native phycobilisomes from the cyanobacterium _Acaryochloris marina_ alongside recombinant regulatory proteins AmNblA and AmNblB. To explore the biochemical mechanisms behind phycobilisome disassembly, we will combine protein interaction assays, spectroscopy, and structural analysis. These complementary approaches will link broad antenna breakdown to specific molecular events—such as whether the regulatory proteins act independently, undergo structural changes, or directly interact with the antenna substrate.
