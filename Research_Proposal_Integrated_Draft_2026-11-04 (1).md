# Molecular Organization and Regulation of Photosynthetic Antenna Systems

**Carlos Alberto Martínez · Technion — Israel Institute of Technology · Supervisor: Prof. Dvir Harris**

## Introduction

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy, driving primary production and sustaining life on Earth (Field et al., 1998). This fundamental process begins with the absorption of light, whose intensity and spectral composition vary across habitats and over time.

Photosynthetic organisms capture light through specialized antenna systems, where proteins organize pigments that absorb photons and transfer excitation energy to reaction centers, which initiate its conversion into chemical energy (Zhao et al., 2024). Light-harvesting antenna complexes (LHC) support photosynthesis across such diverse environments—from dim aquatic habitats and sun-exposed deserts to the interior of host cells and the familiar vegetation of gardens and backyards (Hoppe et al., 2024; Harel et al., 2004; Xiang et al., 2013). Their molecular organization, coupled with mechanisms that regulate their function, abundance, and turnover, enables organisms to capture available light while managing excess excitation (Iglesias-Prieto and Trench, 1997; Li et al., 2000; Hu et al., 2020). Across photosynthetic lineages, antenna systems differ in pigment composition, protein architecture, and assembly organization, reflecting distinct evolutionary histories and ecological contexts (Liu et al., 2004; Zhao et al., 2024). Understanding how these pigment–protein systems assemble and are regulated provides a molecular basis for explaining their light-harvesting properties and their responses to changing cellular requirements.

This research proposal examines the molecular organization and regulation of two distinct photosynthetic antenna systems: in *Dinophyceae*, we will determine how composition and assembly relate to the spectroscopic properties of LHCs; in *Cyanophyceae*, we will examine how regulatory proteins affect LHC organization and disassembly. Together, we address how antenna components form functional molecular assemblies and how regulatory proteins alter those assemblies.

*Symbiodiniaceae* provide a system for investigating how distinct apoprotein–pigment scaffolds organize photosynthetic pigments. Their antenna machinery includes water-soluble peridinin–chlorophyll *a* proteins (PCP) and membrane-associated chlorophyll *a*–chlorophyll $c_2$–peridinin complexes (acpPC) (Jiang et al., 2012, 2014). These antenna systems incorporate overlapping sets of pigments within different molecular architectures, offering a framework to examine how protein composition and assembly influence light-harvesting properties. Previous biochemical studies have identified functional trimeric acpPC assemblies (Jiang et al., 2014), while cryo-electron microscopy has resolved a *Symbiodinium* photosystem I supercomplex surrounded by an organized array of acpPC antenna proteins (Zhao et al., 2024). These findings provide critical reference points for investigating the complexes recovered from the strains examined here. Chapter 1 will investigate which antenna assemblies can be reproducibly isolated from selected *Symbiodiniaceae* and how their molecular organization relates to their absorption and fluorescence properties. Tracking soluble and membrane fractions separately will support the identification of distinct antenna populations, with structural investigations pursued concurrently.

Understanding antenna regulation also requires determining how established pigment–protein assemblies are dismantled (Levi et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020). Chapter 2 will investigate these dismantling processes using native phycobilisomes from the cyanobacterium *Acaryochloris marina* alongside recombinant regulatory proteins AmNblA and AmNblB. To explore the biochemical mechanisms behind phycobilisome disassembly, we will combine protein interaction assays, spectroscopy, and structural analysis. These complementary approaches will link broad antenna breakdown to specific molecular events—such as whether the regulatory proteins act independently, undergo structural changes, or directly interact with the antenna substrate.

This research extends the understanding on how photosynthetic organisms maintain and modify their light-harvesting machinery in diverse environments. In Symbiodiniaceae, antenna maintenance is relevant to the stability of photosynthesis under environmental stress: impaired synthesis of antenna proteins has been linked to heat-induced photobleaching in cultured symbionts (Takahashi et al., 2008). Characterizing their molecular assemblies will provide a foundation for investigating how antenna organization contributes to light utilization and, ultimately, the physiological responses relevant to coral symbioses. In _Acaryochloris marina_, phycobilisomes complement chlorophyll _d_-based photosynthesis by harvesting visible light, and their abundance responds to spectral conditions (Kashimoto et al., 2020). Establishing how these antennae are remodeled will contribute to understanding the regulation of light-harvesting investment in an organism adapted to distinctive light environments. Together, the two systems will provide complementary insight into the molecular diversity and regulation of oxygenic photosynthetic antennae.

The light-harvesting properties of photosynthetic antennae arise from the organization of pigments within protein scaffolds. Pigment chemical identity establishes the underlying electronic transitions available for light absorption, while the surrounding protein environment and interactions with neighboring pigments influence their energies and spectroscopic properties. The apoprotein therefore contributes both to the spatial arrangement of chromophores and to the local environments in which excitation occurs. In the peridinin–chlorophyll _a_ protein (PCP) of _Amphidinium carterae_, structural analysis revealed close arrangements of peridinin molecules around chlorophylls, providing a molecular foundation for investigating carotenoid-to-chlorophyll excitation transfer (Hofmann et al., 1996). Subsequent spectroscopic studies demonstrated how pigment–protein and pigment–pigment interactions contribute to the observed optical properties of this complex (Kleima et al., 2000). Together, these findings establish that understanding antenna function requires knowledge of both molecular composition and pigment organization.

Following photon absorption, excitation energy can migrate between pigments before being emitted, dissipated, or delivered to a reaction center. The probability and timescale of transfer depend on the electronic properties of the participating pigments and their interactions within the assembly. Pigment separation, relative orientation, and the energetic relationship between donor and acceptor states are therefore important determinants of excitation migration. In PCP, combined structural and time-resolved spectroscopic investigations have connected defined pigment arrangements with chlorophyll-to-chlorophyll transfer, while studies of peridinin have identified contributions from excited states with intramolecular charge-transfer character. These observations illustrate how antenna proteins organize a molecular environment that supports specific excitation pathways (Kleima et al., 2000; see also studies of peridinin-mediated transfer).

Absorption spectroscopy interrogates the electronic transitions of the pigment population present in a preparation. Band positions, widths, and relative amplitudes provide information about pigment-associated spectral features and their environments. For chlorophyll-containing antennae, the Qy region is particularly informative about low-energy chlorophyll transitions, whereas peridinin contributes prominently in the blue-green region. However, a spectral difference can arise from changes in pigment composition, local interactions, or the relative abundance of distinct populations. Consequently, a shifted absorption maximum is a starting point for investigating a molecular change rather than a unique assignment of its cause. Studies of PCP combining structural information with several spectroscopic measurements illustrate how such assignments can be constrained by independent evidence (Kleima et al., 2000).

Fluorescence measurements provide complementary information about the fate of excitation after absorption. An emission spectrum records the distribution of emitted light following excitation at a selected wavelength. A fluorescence excitation spectrum instead varies the excitation wavelength while monitoring emission within a selected band, revealing which absorbing populations contribute to that emission. For a characterized antenna preparation, comparison of corrected excitation and absorption spectra can therefore assess the contribution of accessory-pigment absorption to chlorophyll fluorescence and, under appropriate assumptions, estimate transfer efficiency. Jiang et al. (2012) applied this approach to PCP from _Symbiodinium_. Resolving the timescales and pathways underlying that transfer requires additional temporal information, such as time-resolved fluorescence or transient absorption measurements. Thus, steady-state and time-resolved spectroscopy address complementary aspects of antenna function: the spectral outcome of excitation and the dynamics that produce it.

The relationship between organization and optical behavior also extends to interactions among pigment–protein subunits. In _Acaryochloris marina_, investigation of isolated phycobilisomes under conditions that preserved assemblies or promoted their dissociation revealed assembly-dependent differences in fluorescence behavior. Combined time-resolved absorption and fluorescence analyses further supported the presence of spectrally distinct phycocyanobilin populations and informed models of excitation migration within the antenna (Niedzwiedzki et al., 2019). These results demonstrate the importance of characterizing the assembly present during a spectroscopic measurement and of considering solution conditions when interpreting its optical properties.

These principles motivate an integrated investigation of antenna composition, assembly, and spectroscopy. Protein identification establishes which components are present; biochemical fractionation and particle characterization distinguish recoverable molecular populations; and structural analysis can reveal the contacts and pigment arrangements underlying their organization. Spectroscopy then examines the optical behavior associated with those characterized populations. In the present research, this framework will support two complementary questions: how the organization of isolated Symbiodiniaceae antenna complexes relates to their absorption and fluorescence properties, and how defined regulatory proteins alter the organization and optical behavior of _A. marina_ phycobilisomes.

### Introduction to methods

**Chromatographic separation and characterization of pigment–protein complexes**

Liquid chromatography separates components according to differences in their distribution or interactions between a flowing mobile phase and a stationary phase. For pigment–protein complexes, chromatography serves both preparative and analytical purposes: it provides fractions for subsequent experiments and reveals differences in their behavior under defined solution conditions. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) and ion-exchange chromatography (IEX) provide complementary separations based primarily on hydrodynamic size and electrostatic interactions, respectively. Combining these modes can help resolve populations that overlap in one property but differ in another.

**ÄKTA instrumentation and signal acquisition**

ÄKTA systems provide controlled delivery of mobile phases through a chromatographic column, together with sample introduction, monitoring, and fraction collection. The separation mechanism is determined by the column resin and solution conditions. Pumps establish flow and, in gradient-capable configurations, vary the proportions of mobile phases. Injection introduces the sample into the flow path, while downstream detectors record the eluting material. Detector signals are displayed against time or elution volume, allowing individual fractions to be related to the chromatographic profile.

The detector-to-collector delay volume is important because material passing through the detector reaches the collection vessel later. Accounting for this volume aligns collected fractions with the corresponding peaks. For a constant volumetric flow rate, (Q), the transit time associated with a delay volume, (V_d), is (t_d=V_d/Q). Changes to tubing or downstream modules can therefore change the relationship between detection and collection.

Absorbance monitoring follows light attenuation by the eluting sample. Protein-associated ultraviolet absorbance and pigment-associated visible absorbance can provide complementary information, where the installed detector supports the required wavelengths. A chromatographic absorbance peak represents the combined contributions of absorbing species passing through the flow cell. Its height depends on concentration and optical response, while peak area integrates that response over the elution interval. Consequently, comparisons of pigment-containing fractions will require consideration of wavelength-dependent extinction coefficients, baseline contributions, and possible light scattering. Visible spectra of collected fractions will complement online detection where necessary.

**Size-exclusion chromatography: hydrodynamic behavior and molecular organization**

SEC separates molecules through differential access to the pores of resin particles. Large particles are excluded from a greater proportion of the pore volume and travel through the column more rapidly. Smaller particles access more of the internal volume and elute later. Separation ideally occurs without appreciable binding to the stationary phase.

The relevant molecular property is hydrodynamic size, which depends on molecular shape and hydration as well as mass. The hydrodynamic radius can be understood as the radius of an equivalent sphere with the same translational friction. For a spherical particle under the assumptions of the Stokes–Einstein relationship,

[  
D=\frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{6\pi\eta R_{\mathrm h}},  
]

where (D) is the translational diffusion coefficient, (k_{\mathrm B}) the Boltzmann constant, (T) the absolute temperature, (\eta) the solvent viscosity, and (R_{\mathrm h}) the hydrodynamic radius. SEC does not directly measure diffusion; calibration against suitable standards connects retention behavior with hydrodynamic size. This distinction explains why proteins of equal mass can elute differently when their shapes differ.

Retention is commonly expressed using the distribution coefficient:

[  
K_{\mathrm{av}}=\frac{V_e-V_0}{V_t-V_0},  
]

where (V_e) is the sample elution volume, (V_0) the void volume accessible to fully excluded particles, and (V_t) the total packed-bed volume. Within the resin’s useful fractionation range, calibration with appropriate standards can relate (K_{\mathrm{av}}) to apparent molecular mass or hydrodynamic radius. Particles excluded from the pores elute near (V_0), where their individual sizes are poorly resolved.

For membrane-derived antennae, the chromatographic particle may include associated detergent and lipids. An elongated assembly or a protein–detergent complex can therefore behave differently from a globular protein standard of comparable protein mass. SEC will consequently provide evidence about population size and heterogeneity, with oligomeric assignments supported by composition and complementary measurements. Jiang et al. (2014), for example, combined SEC, native electrophoresis, native mass spectrometry, and pigment analysis to support a trimeric assignment for an isolated _Symbiodinium_ acpPC preparation.

Resolution depends on both differences in retention and the widths of the eluting bands. Sample volume, column dimensions, resin properties, flow rate, and dispersion outside the column all influence peak broadening. Excessive injection volume can obscure populations with similar retention, while unsuitable flow conditions can compromise separation efficiency. Method development will therefore balance resolution, sample recovery, and separation time within the operating limits of the selected column.

**Ion-exchange chromatography: protonation and electrostatic interactions**

IEX separates proteins through reversible interactions with charged ligands on a resin. Anion exchangers contain positively charged groups that bind negatively charged species; cation exchangers contain negatively charged groups that bind positively charged species. The protonation of protein functional groups depends on pH, making buffer pH a major determinant of retention.

The isoelectric point, pI, is the pH at which the protein has zero net charge. As an initial guide, a protein is generally net negative above its pI and net positive below it. However, binding also depends on the distribution and accessibility of surface charges. The pI therefore guides condition selection without uniquely predicting chromatographic behavior. Strong and weak ion exchangers differ in the pH dependence of their ligand ionization; these terms do not simply describe stronger or weaker binding to every protein.

Bound proteins can be eluted by increasing salt concentration or changing pH. During salt-gradient elution, mobile-phase ions compete with protein–resin interactions and alter the electrostatic environment. During pH-gradient elution, changes in protein protonation alter retention. A shallower gradient can improve separation of closely retained populations, although this increases elution volume and may dilute the recovered material. Buffering must also maintain the intended pH throughout the separation, because changes in pH can alter selectivity independently of the programmed salt gradient.

For pigment–protein assemblies, conditions supporting chromatographic binding must also preserve the molecular population of interest. Sample pH and conductivity will therefore be matched to suitable loading conditions, and any buffer exchange will be evaluated for its effect on assembly stability. This consideration is especially relevant when a preparation requires relatively high salt concentrations for preservation but lower ionic strength for effective binding. The order of purification steps will follow the compatibility of these requirements.

**Application to antenna characterization and disassembly**

In Chapter 1, chromatography will resolve and recover soluble and membrane-derived antenna populations for protein identification, spectroscopic analysis, and structural investigation. SEC will examine hydrodynamic distributions, while IEX may distinguish populations with different charge-dependent retention. Differences between fractions will be assigned to composition or organization through subsequent characterization.

In Chapter 2, compatible chromatographic separations may reveal redistribution of AmPBS-derived material following exposure to AmNblA or AmNblB. A shift toward later SEC elution would motivate investigation of smaller hydrodynamic populations; a shift toward earlier elution would motivate investigation of larger assemblies or aggregates. Interpretation will also consider material recovery and whether the separation itself changes the substrate. Chromatography will thus connect an observed population change with recoverable material that can be examined for protein composition, pigment association, and optical behavior.

# Chapter 1 — Molecular Organization of Photosynthetic Antennae in Symbiodiniaceae

## 1.1 Background and rationale

Symbiodiniaceae encompass physiologically diverse dinoflagellates. Work with cultured isolates has demonstrated differences in trophic capabilities and compatibility with a common animal host (Xiang et al., 2013). These differences provide biological context for investigating antenna organization, while the experimental focus of this chapter is the pigment–protein material that can be isolated, identified and characterized from available cultures.

The available panel comprises five strains:

| Strain | Species |
|---|---|
| SSA01 | *Symbiodinium linucheae* |
| SSA02 | *Symbiodinium necroappetens* |
| SSA03 | *Symbiodinium pilosum* |
| SSB01 | *Breviolum minutum* |
| SSE01 | *Effrenium voratum* |

These cultures provide the starting material for investigating antenna composition, molecular organization and spectroscopic properties. The strain used for initial isolation and purification will be selected as preparatory work establishes biomass availability and the reproducibility of antenna recovery. Soluble and membrane fractions will initially be tracked separately. Subsequent comparisons among strains will be guided by the availability of reproducible, characterized antenna fractions; the panel does not require equally deep characterization of all five strains.

Soluble PCP and membrane-associated acpPC provide distinct molecular contexts for organizing peridinin and chlorophyll. Jiang et al. (2012) characterized PCP from *Symbiodinium*, whereas Jiang et al. (2014) combined native mass spectrometry, size-exclusion chromatography and native electrophoresis to support a trimeric organization of an isolated acpPC preparation. These results provide biochemical references, but do not establish the assembly state of material isolated from the current cultures.

Larger photosystem-associated assemblies provide a second level of organization. Kato et al. (2020) isolated a giant PSI supercomplex from *Breviolum minutum*. Zhao et al. (2024) resolved a *Symbiodinium* PSI complex surrounded by 13 AcpPCI antenna proteins, and Li et al. (2024) reported distinct PSI–AcpPCI organizations in dinoflagellates. These studies establish the feasibility of recovering and structurally examining membrane antenna assemblies. Species-level correspondence with an available culture is a useful precedent, but exact isolate equivalence and comparable purification behavior cannot be assumed.

Identifying the recovered proteins is particularly important because acpPC is a diverse protein family. Boldt et al. (2012) documented divergent integral light-harvesting protein sequences in *Symbiodinium*, including polyprotein organization. Consequently, a colored fraction, apparent gel band or chlorophyll spectrum alone cannot identify an acpPC subunit. Protein assignments must distinguish family-level identification from uniquely supported subunits and unresolved protein groups.

Culture irradiance and light history will be documented for each preparation because irradiance can influence chlorophyll–protein distributions (Iglesias-Prieto and Trench, 1997). The experimental strategy will focus on isolating and characterizing molecular assemblies from the available cultures.

Symbiodiniaceae possess two structurally distinct classes of peridinin-containing light-harvesting antennae: water-soluble peridinin–chlorophyll _a_ proteins (PCP) and membrane-integral chlorophyll _a_–chlorophyll _c₂_–peridinin proteins (acpPC). Both incorporate peridinin and chlorophyll _a_, whereas acpPC additionally incorporates chlorophyll _c₂_. Their overlapping pigment composition occurs within different protein scaffolds and physical environments, providing complementary systems for investigating how pigment binding, protein identity, and assembly organization contribute to light-harvesting properties. This distinction also establishes two experimentally separable sources of antenna material: soluble pigment–protein complexes and complexes recovered through membrane solubilization (Boldt et al., 2012; Jiang et al., 2012, 2014).

PCP provides a well-established example of how a soluble protein scaffold organizes carotenoids around chlorophyll acceptors. In the structurally characterized main-form PCP of _Amphidinium carterae_, each polypeptide contains two related domains that collectively bind eight peridinins and two chlorophyll _a_ molecules; three polypeptides associate in the crystallographic assembly (Hofmann et al., 1996). This architecture provides a reference for understanding pigment organization, although its oligomeric arrangement cannot automatically be assigned to PCP preparations from other dinoflagellates. Work on _Symbiodinium_ PCP combined protein identification with biochemical and spectroscopic characterization, establishing efficient peridinin-to-chlorophyll excitation transfer in an isolated preparation (Jiang et al., 2012). PCP therefore offers a tractable system in which molecular identity and optical behavior can be examined together, with the assembly state of each recovered preparation established experimentally.

Membrane-associated acpPC introduces an additional level of organizational complexity because antenna proteins can participate in oligomeric assemblies and associate with photosystem cores. Jiang et al. (2014) isolated an acpPC preparation for which size-exclusion chromatography, blue-native electrophoresis, native mass spectrometry, and pigment analysis collectively supported a trimeric organization. The significance of this study lies in the convergence of independent measurements: the proposed assembly was supported by evidence concerning both its constituent polypeptides and the intact pigment-containing complex. Such preparations establish that discrete acpPC assemblies can be recovered and characterized, while leaving open which populations will be obtained from other isolates and under different extraction conditions.

Recent cryo-electron microscopy studies have extended this understanding from isolated antenna assemblies to photosystem-associated architectures. Zhao et al. (2024) resolved a _Symbiodinium_ PSI supercomplex containing 13 peripheral AcpPCI antenna proteins and identified specific contacts linking antenna subunits to one another and to the PSI core. Li et al. (2024) resolved PSI–AcpPCI complexes from _Amphidinium carterae_ and _Symbiodinium_, revealing distinct organizations of the peripheral antennae. These structures establish molecular reference points for investigating antenna–antenna and antenna–photosystem interfaces, as well as pigment arrangements that may support excitation transfer across those interfaces. They also show why acpPC should be considered across several organizational levels, from individual pigment-binding proteins to larger assemblies. Here, AcpPCI denotes antenna proteins identified in PSI-associated complexes; assignment of an isolated fraction to this category will require evidence for its association with PSI.

A further challenge is the diversity of the proteins underlying these assemblies. Boldt et al. (2012) documented divergent acpPC-related sequences and polyprotein organization in _Symbiodinium_. This diversity raises a question that cannot be resolved through pigment spectra alone: which members of the antenna-protein family contribute to a particular recovered population? Sequence diversity identifies potential molecular components, but does not by itself establish their expression, incorporation into a complex, or functional specialization. Protein-level identification is therefore central to interpreting differences among preparations. Where related proteins share peptide sequences, assignments will need to distinguish uniquely supported subunits from unresolved protein groups. Linking these assignments to assembly measurements will provide a basis for asking whether separable antenna populations differ in protein composition, molecular organization, or both.

Together, these studies motivate investigation of the relationship between the composition of recovered antennae, their assembly state, and their optical properties. Comparisons between PCP and acpPC will involve differences in pigment complement as well as protein architecture; consequently, any spectral difference will require interpretation in the context of both. Within a characterized antenna population, controlled changes in solution conditions or sample handling will provide a more focused means of examining whether altered organization accompanies changes in absorption or fluorescence. Complementary measurements will help distinguish such responses from changes in composition, aggregation, or loss of material.

Chapter 1 will therefore investigate which antenna assemblies can be reproducibly recovered from the available Symbiodiniaceae cultures, what molecular components constitute those assemblies, and how their organization relates to their spectroscopic properties. Initial material will be selected according to biomass availability and reproducible recovery of characterized complexes. Detailed comparisons and structural investigations will follow the properties of the recovered material, allowing the research to address defined molecular questions without presupposing a particular strain, oligomeric state, or photosystem-associated architecture.

## 1.2 Research question and objectives

**Central question:** What is the molecular organization of native photosynthetic antenna complexes in selected Symbiodiniaceae, and how does that organization relate to their spectroscopic properties?

Published structures and biochemical preparations provide reference architectures. This chapter will establish which assemblies are recovered from the available cultures, their preparation reproducibility, and the molecular and optical properties of each characterized fraction.

The chapter has three objectives:

1. **Isolate and identify antenna complexes.** Establish reproducible recovery of soluble and membrane-associated pigment–protein fractions and determine their protein identities.
2. **Characterize molecular organization.** Determine the supported composition, assembly state and heterogeneity of selected preparations, incorporating structural analysis where sample quality permits.
3. **Relate organization to spectroscopic properties.** Compare absorption and fluorescence of characterized fractions and test selected questions about their stability or organization through controlled perturbations.

The first milestone is **a reproducible antenna fraction characterized by protein identity, pigment-associated spectral features and assembly state**. Detailed pigment identities and stoichiometries require an additional validated analytical method; they are not assumed achievable from absorption spectra alone.

## 1.3 Preliminary progress and experimental starting point

The five cultures have been established and propagated, including scale-up to approximately 3 L in 5-L flasks. Growth monitoring has included visual observations and cuvette-based OD₇₅₀ measurements using a NanoDrop 2000c. Methanol-based pigment extraction, exploratory flow cytometry and confocal imaging have also been undertaken, providing a foundation for culture monitoring and optical characterization.

SSA03 imaging and a sonication series provide an initial basis for developing cell-disruption and microscopy workflows. Subsequent optimization will evaluate disruption efficiency together with recovery and preservation of native pigment–protein assemblies.

Maintenance and production cultures will be managed separately. Medium, temperature, photoperiod, photon flux density, growth phase and harvest conditions will be recorded for each preparation to support reproducibility and interpretation of differences among samples.

ÄKTA chromatography and steady-state spectroscopy will provide the principal purification and optical characterization platform. Chromatographic modes and protein-identification methods will be selected to suit the recovered fractions and the analytical requirements of each objective.

## 1.4 Experimental strategy

### Objective 1 — Isolate and identify antenna complexes

Initial pilots will connect sample preparation to a recorded recovery chain: culture volume and a validated biomass descriptor, harvested cells, soluble and membrane fractions, solubilized membrane material, and collected chromatographic fractions. OD₇₅₀ will be retained as a longitudinal culture measurement; conversion to cell number or biomass requires calibration. Production volume will follow measured recovery rather than a predetermined large-scale target.

Cell disruption will be evaluated by microscopy together with pigment/protein partitioning and preservation of recoverable complexes. Mechanical disruption conditions, processing temperature and harvest/storage buffers will be optimized to balance disruption efficiency with antenna preservation. Soluble and particulate fractions from the same harvest will be retained to assess recovery of both antenna classes.

Membrane-associated material will undergo controlled solubilization and fractionation informed by published preparations (Jovine et al., 1995; Kato et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2024). The variables to document include detergent identity and concentration, detergent-to-sample ratio, ionic conditions, processing time, temperature and concentration steps. ÄKTA chromatography will support separation and collection of fractions; the choice and order of chromatographic modes will follow column availability and preparation behavior. Native electrophoresis or density-based separations may provide complementary evidence where suitable.

Antenna identity will be established through protein-level analysis, using LC-MS/MS where suitable analytical access and sequence coverage permit. Gel profiles will track enrichment, while protein assignments will use sequence evidence and distinguish uniquely identified subunits from shared-peptide groups. Published homologous sequences will provide reference information. Genome or transcriptome sequencing may be used if additional sequence information is required for reliable identification.

Fractions without adequate identity evidence will be described operationally, for example as pigment-rich soluble or membrane fractions. Assignment as PSI–AcpPCI will additionally require evidence for PSI-core components and their association with antenna proteins; co-detection in a heterogeneous mixture is insufficient by itself. A validated PCP preparation can support selected optical comparisons, but does not validate membrane extraction.

Whole-cell flow cytometry will support culture monitoring and sample preparation, informed by published methods (Krediet et al., 2015; Anthony et al., 2023). Instrument settings and counting procedures will be validated for the cultures examined. Autofluorescence and scatter will be interpreted as optical phenotypes, with independent assays used for viability or antenna-composition assignments. Potential contamination will be assessed when it could compromise attribution of purified components.

To mitigate the risk of detergent-induced dissociation of fragile supercomplexes during extraction, pilot screens will evaluate a panel of mild non-ionic and zwitterionic detergents (such as DDM and digitonin) across varying concentrations.

### Objective 2 — Characterize molecular organization

Selected fractions will be examined using complementary measurements of composition, apparent size and heterogeneity. Chromatographic behavior and native electrophoresis will be interpreted in the context of detergent, shape and possible co-eluting species. Apparent size will not automatically be converted into an oligomeric stoichiometry. Repeated preparations will determine whether a population is recovered consistently and whether its distribution changes during processing or storage.

Structural investigation will be pursued for stable, sufficiently homogeneous preparations. Cryo-EM feasibility will be assessed for suitable assemblies, and structural interpretation will be anchored in independently established composition and sample behavior. The analysis will address protein contacts, pigment arrangement and assembly interfaces to the extent supported by map resolution and particle homogeneity.

Mass photometry may provide complementary measurements of compatible particles. Sample concentration, buffer composition and detergent background will be evaluated to establish measurement reliability and reproducibility.

### Objective 3 — Relate organization to spectroscopic properties

Absorption and steady-state fluorescence measurements will be made on matched, characterized fractions. Comparisons will document excitation wavelength, acquisition settings, concentration, buffer/detergent composition, temperature and time after preparation. Normalization will be selected to answer the particular question—for example, distinguishing recoverable material abundance from differences in spectral shape—and raw intensity information will be retained where needed for that distinction.

Methanol extraction and absorption spectroscopy will provide pigment-associated spectral information using validated extraction conditions and solvent-compatible optics. Assignment of individual pigments and quantitative stoichiometry will require complementary analytical methods validated with appropriate standards.

Once a characterized preparation is available, controlled changes in solution conditions or sample handling will test a specific assembly or stability question. Matched controls and complementary measurements will distinguish assembly changes from material loss, aggregation or altered optical behavior. Reversibility will be evaluated where feasible.

Steady-state spectra can reveal differences in absorption and emission associated with characterized fractions. They do not independently resolve excited-state lifetimes, energy-transfer rates or atomic pigment geometry. Time-resolved spectroscopy remains a conditional extension requiring confirmed access and a defined question; the core objective remains the relationship between molecular organization and measured absorption/fluorescence properties.

Spectroscopic tracking will specifically monitor shifts and amplitudes in chlorophyll and carotenoid Qy absorption bands, as well as changes in fluorescence emission spectra, to detect uncoupling or energy-transfer alterations between antenna complexes and reaction centers.

## 1.5 Replication, interpretation and anticipated outcomes

Independent cultures and independently processed preparations will underpin confirmatory comparisons. Repeat readings or injections from the same preparation will constitute technical replicates. Pilot estimates of variability will inform replication, effect-size thresholds and preparation-quality criteria before confirmatory experiments. Screening observations will be reassessed using independently prepared material.

The anticipated outcome is a set of reproducibly recovered, identified antenna preparations with supported assembly assignments and associated spectra. If several populations are resolved, their differences can be examined in terms of composition, assembly or both. If only one preparation is sufficiently reproducible, its detailed characterization can still address the central question. Broader strain comparisons will be added when supported by preparation quality and a specific question.

Failure to recover an intact complex will first be treated as a limitation of extraction or preservation, rather than evidence that the antenna is absent from the organism. Similar spectra among well-characterized fractions are also informative and will constrain the proposed relationship between their molecular differences and optical properties. Comparisons will apply to the tested isolates; the five-strain panel alone does not support genus-wide evolutionary claims.

# Chapter 2 — Molecular Regulation of Phycobilisome Disassembly in *Acaryochloris marina*

## 2.1 Background and rationale

Native phycobilisomes from *Acaryochloris marina* (AmPBS), together with recombinant AmNblA and AmNblB, provide a tractable system for investigating antenna disassembly. Characterization of these components will establish the basis for controlled reconstitution and mechanistic experiments.

Work in other cyanobacteria establishes several relevant functions. In *Synechocystis* PCC 6803, NblA proteins participate in substrate recruitment to Clp-dependent proteolysis (Baier et al., 2014). Levi et al. (2018) reported an NblA-dependent role for NblB in cell-free phycobilisome decomposition. Hu et al. (2020) found that NblA and NblB influence phycobiliprotein dissociation and chromophore-related reactions, including reactions involving bilin lyases; their assays did not detect direct NblA–NblB interaction. These findings motivate tests of substrate-dependent cooperation, but neither establish the same mechanism in *A. marina* nor exclude transient or substrate-associated interactions.

The involvement of NblD in phycobilisome degradation further demonstrates that the cellular pathway includes additional factors (Krauspe et al., 2021). The minimal reconstruction here is intended to establish what native AmPBS and recombinant AmNblA/AmNblB can do under defined conditions. Extensions involving heterologous NblD, ClpC and associated proteolytic machinery, or bilin lyases will enter through specific mechanistic questions.

Previous work provides a structural and functional foundation for investigating phycobilisome remodeling in _Acaryochloris marina_. Suissa Szlejf (2025) characterized native AmPBS through biochemical fractionation, spectroscopy, proteomics and cryo-electron microscopy, revealing phycocyanin isoforms and an unusual organization of linker proteins. In complementary cellular experiments, expression of AmNblA in an _nblA_-deficient _Synechococcus elongatus_ PCC 7942 strain restored a nitrogen-starvation-associated bleaching and phycobilisome-disassembly phenotype. These findings motivate examination of AmNblA activity against its native antenna substrate under defined biochemical conditions. Building on this foundation, the present study will determine how recombinant AmNblA and AmNblB individually and jointly affect native AmPBS, and whether their functional relationship involves selective substrate remodeling, direct association or substrate-mediated cooperation.

Phycobilisomes are pigment–protein assemblies whose organization connects light absorption with excitation transfer. Their phycobiliprotein subunits carry covalently attached bilin chromophores and associate into larger structures. Consequently, antenna breakdown can involve changes at several molecular levels: disruption of interactions between subunits, alteration or detachment of chromophores, and degradation of the polypeptides themselves. These transformations affect different features of the antenna and need not occur simultaneously. Establishing their relationship is central to understanding how regulatory proteins convert an assembled light-harvesting complex into substrates for subsequent turnover (Levi et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020).

_Acaryochloris marina_ provides a distinctive system for investigating this process. Its phycobiliprotein antenna complements chlorophyll _d_-based photosynthesis, extending light harvesting into the visible spectrum. In strain MBIC11017, acclimation to orange light involves changes in phycobilisome abundance and cellular organization, demonstrating that investment in this antenna responds to the spectral environment (Kashimoto et al., 2020). Structural and spectroscopic investigations have described a compact, rod-shaped phycobilisome and heterogeneity among its phycocyanin components, including spectral properties relevant to transfer toward chlorophyll _d_-containing photosystems (Bar-Zvi et al., 2018). These features make AmPBS a useful substrate for examining how changes within a defined antenna assembly influence its molecular and optical behavior.

Recent work in the laboratory provides a more specific structural foundation. Suissa Szlejf (2025) investigated native AmPBS through biochemical fractionation, spectroscopy, proteomics, and cryo-electron microscopy. The thesis reported an unusual arrangement of linker proteins, including placement of CpcD within the rod rather than at its end, and raised questions concerning how these linkers organize and connect the phycocyanin assemblies. This architecture motivates investigation of which interfaces are altered during disassembly. Whether AmNblA or AmNblB affects these interfaces directly, or produces changes elsewhere that destabilize the assembly, remains to be determined.

The physiological importance of phycobilisome turnover is established by studies of cyanobacterial acclimation to nutrient limitation. Under nitrogen starvation, degradation of phycobiliproteins contributes to the redistribution of cellular resources, while loss of antenna pigments changes light-harvesting capacity. Genetic studies have identified several proteins required for this response. However, a nonbleaching phenotype establishes that a factor is needed for normal turnover under the tested conditions; determining its biochemical role requires additional evidence about substrate recognition, molecular interactions, and the transformations it promotes (Collier and Grossman, 1994; Krauspe et al., 2021).

NblA provides an example of how a small protein can confer specificity on a degradation pathway. In _Synechocystis_ sp. PCC 6803, Baier et al. (2014) demonstrated formation of an NblA1/NblA2 heterodimer and its association with phycobiliproteins and ClpC. These findings support an adaptor function in which NblA connects an antenna substrate with the ATP-dependent proteolytic machinery. ClpC serves as the chaperone partner of the Clp protease system, while proteolytic cleavage requires its associated protease components. This distinction separates substrate recognition and recruitment from peptide-bond cleavage. It also leaves a mechanistic question relevant to the present study: whether NblA binding itself changes antenna organization or substrate accessibility before proteolysis occurs.

NblB introduces a complementary question concerning the fate of the pigment–protein linkage. Levi et al. (2018) demonstrated a requirement for NblB in phycobiliprotein turnover in _Synechococcus elongatus_ and reported NblA-dependent phycocyanin loss in a cell-free assay. Their reconstruction combined cyanobacterial soluble extracts with lysates containing recombinant NblB, using extraction conditions intended to produce smaller phycocyanin assemblies. The results supported a role for NblB in pigment breakdown and a proposed involvement in chromophore detachment. They also motivated further experiments to distinguish the contribution of NblB from other extract components and to establish how NblA enables its activity. These questions are particularly relevant when moving from extract-based observations to a defined system containing native antenna substrate and purified regulatory proteins.

Hu et al. (2020) further demonstrated that the relationship between antenna dissociation and chromophore chemistry is complex. Their in vitro experiments showed that NblA and NblB could promote dissociation of phycobiliprotein assemblies and modulate reactions involving bilin attachment, detachment, and transfer. The effects depended on the substrates and reaction partners, including bilin lyases. Direct NblA–NblB interaction was not detected in their assays, although both proteins interacted with other components. These findings motivate models in which functional cooperation occurs through changes to a shared substrate or through additional partners. They also establish the importance of distinguishing the observed effect on an antenna preparation from the particular molecular reaction responsible for that effect.

Evidence from _A. marina_ NblA provides a direct connection to the proposed investigation. Suissa Szlejf (2025) reported that expression of AmNblA in an _nblA_-deficient _S. elongatus_ PCC 7942 background restored a nitrogen-starvation-associated bleaching response and a phycobilisome fractionation pattern resembling that of the wild type. This heterologous complementation supports the ability of AmNblA to participate in a cellular antenna-turnover pathway. Its activity against native AmPBS, and its functional relationship with AmNblB, remain questions for biochemical investigation. Additional factors identified in other cyanobacteria, such as NblD, demonstrate that cellular turnover can require components beyond NblA and NblB; their involvement in the _A. marina_ system will require independent evidence (Krauspe et al., 2021).

Chapter 2 will therefore distinguish changes in assembly organization, chromophore association or chemical state, and polypeptide integrity when native AmPBS is exposed to AmNblA, AmNblB, or both proteins. Measurements of particle populations and biochemical fractions will address antenna remodeling, spectroscopy will track associated optical changes, and complementary pigment and protein analyses will investigate their molecular basis. Order-of-addition and interaction experiments will then test whether the observed activities are consistent with persistent substrate remodeling, direct protein association, simultaneous cooperation, or independent effects. This approach will establish which transformations the defined components can promote under the tested conditions and provide a basis for identifying additional requirements when motivated by the results.

## 2.2 Research question and objectives

**Central question:** How do AmNblA and AmNblB individually and jointly alter native *Acaryochloris marina* phycobilisomes, and what molecular interactions account for those effects?

The chapter has three objectives:

1. **Characterize the starting components:** establish identity, integrity and assembly behavior of native AmPBS and the recombinant proteins used in each assay.
2. **Establish individual and combined effects:** determine whether AmNblA, AmNblB or their combination produces reproducible changes in antenna organization, spectra or protein integrity.
3. **Explain the relationship between their activities:** distinguish substrate remodeling, simultaneous cooperation, direct protein association and independent activity using order-of-addition, fractionation, interaction and structure-informed experiments.

The working models remain alternatives. A stable AmNblA-generated intermediate, direct AmNblA–AmNblB binding and autonomous chromophore removal by AmNblB are not assumed.

## 2.3 Preliminary progress and experimental starting point

Native AmPBS and recombinant AmNblA and AmNblB provide the laboratory starting materials. NblB transformation has been successful, and crystals have been obtained. These preparations will support biochemical characterization and crystallographic investigation.

AmPBS concentration and cryo-EM sample preparation have been undertaken, and a sample has been submitted for cryo-EM analysis. Substrate integrity, stability and usable concentration will be characterized before establishing the biochemical assay matrix.

## 2.4 Experimental strategy

### Objective 1 — Characterize starting components

Each assay series will document the native substrate preparation and the recombinant constructs, including tags, purification history, storage and concentration determination. Protein integrity and purity will be examined by appropriate biochemical analysis; chromatography and spectroscopy will describe assembly behavior and the optical baseline. A matched substrate-only control will establish spontaneous change over the assay period.

Assay buffers must preserve the substrate while remaining compatible with the added proteins and measurements. Buffer carried with each recombinant protein will be matched in the corresponding controls. Protein quality is necessary but does not establish biological activity: binding and remodeling will be measured separately where possible, and a binding result alone will not be called disassembly.

Structural analysis of AmNblB will begin with diffraction assessment of the existing crystals. An experimental structure, if obtained, will guide hypotheses about substrate recognition, interfaces and oligomeric organization. Computational models may provide complementary hypotheses for experimental testing.

### Objective 2 — Establish individual and combined effects

The initial comparison will contain four substrate conditions:

| Condition | Question |
|---|---|
| AmPBS alone | How stable is the substrate under the assay conditions? |
| AmPBS + AmNblA | What effect is attributable to AmNblA? |
| AmPBS + AmNblB | What effect is attributable to AmNblB? |
| AmPBS + AmNblA + AmNblB | Does the combined response differ from either individual response? |

Protein-only and matched-buffer controls will identify optical or particle signals contributed by the added components. Initial stability and concentration-response experiments will guide the selection of molar ratios, incubation times and temperatures. Replication will be established using pilot variability before confirmatory comparisons.

Three outcomes will be distinguished throughout:

| Outcome | Evidence sought | Interpretation limit |
|---|---|---|
| Assembly remodeling or disassembly | Redistribution of antenna populations by compatible chromatography, native separation and/or mass photometry, with compositional support | Loss from a soluble fraction may reflect aggregation or precipitation |
| Chromophore alteration or release | Evidence concerning pigment association and chemical state alongside spectral changes | A fluorescence decrease alone does not establish chromophore detachment |
| Protein degradation | Changes in intact polypeptides or supported cleavage products | Disassembly is not equivalent to proteolysis |

The Refeyn TwoMP can be evaluated for changes in sufficiently large, measurable AmPBS-derived populations; detecting free AmNblA is not the primary purpose. Dilution into the measurement conditions may itself affect assembly and must be controlled. Fluorolog-3 spectra will follow optical changes, and ÄKTA fractionation may recover populations for subsequent composition and activity assays, provided the separation preserves them.

A discrete intermediate is one possible result. Heterogeneous disassembly, a spectral response without a resolved size shift, or no reproducible effect are also possible. Each outcome will determine which follow-up experiments are informative.

### Objective 3 — Explain the relationship between AmNblA and AmNblB

Order-of-addition experiments will compare simultaneous addition with AmNblA pretreatment followed by AmNblB, and, where informative, the reverse sequence. The final composition, elapsed time and handling will be matched so that order is not confounded with exposure or buffer changes.

Testing a persistent substrate change requires more than adding AmNblB after AmNblA. If a treated fraction is isolated, residual free and substrate-associated AmNblA must be assessed. A mock-treated AmPBS sample will undergo the same separation, and the treated substrate's stability during separation will be checked. A residual-AmNblA control or titration will test whether carryover could explain the subsequent response. If AmNblA cannot be separated without changing the substrate, the experiment will be reported as sequential exposure, not proof of activity on an AmNblA-free intermediate.

| Mechanistic model | Observation that would support it | Additional evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Sequential substrate remodeling | Pretreatment changes the subsequent AmNblB response after relevant carryover and processing controls | Identity and persistence of the altered substrate state |
| Direct AmNblA–AmNblB association | A reproducible interaction is detected in a suitable assay | Evidence that the interaction contributes to the functional response |
| Simultaneous or substrate-mediated cooperation | The combined response requires both factors under the tested conditions | Association with substrate and tests separating cooperation from simple additive effects |
| Parallel activity | Each factor produces a response with limited dependence on prior exposure to the other | Sufficient assay sensitivity and comparisons across informative conditions |
| Additional-component dependence | A defined added component restores or changes a response absent from the minimal system | Controls for protein quality, substrate suitability and the added component's own effect |

Direct interaction assays may include controlled pull-down/co-elution experiments or another validated solution-based method. Tag, matrix and nonspecific-binding controls will be included as appropriate. A negative result will be bounded by detection limits; a positive interaction will not alone establish the disassembly mechanism. A larger effect with both proteins will not be described as synergy without a defined quantitative reference for combined activity.

Structural information will guide targeted substitutions after a reproducible functional assay is established. Residues will be selected based on candidate interfaces or substrate-recognition features. Mutant integrity and assembly behavior will be assessed before interpreting changes in binding or remodeling as specific mechanistic effects.

Chromophore-fate analysis will combine validated bilin-sensitive gel measurements with comparisons of protein-associated and extractable spectral signals. Chemical identity and covalent-bond cleavage will be investigated through complementary pigment or chromopeptide analyses where suitable methods are accessible and validated.

## 2.5 Anticipated outcomes and alternatives

The intended contribution is an experimentally supported account of which transformations AmNblA and AmNblB promote individually and together, and which models explain their relationship. Even if no stable intermediate can be isolated, reproducible changes in substrate distributions may be investigated. If no effect is detected, protein quality, substrate state, assay conditions and sensitivity will be evaluated before proposing a missing factor. A null result alone does not prove that an additional component is required.

Heterologous NblD can test a defined cross-species biochemical question, with its organism of origin stated. ClpC-dependent proteolysis requires consideration of the relevant protease components and ATP-dependent assay requirements; ClpC alone will not be treated as the complete degradation system. Bilin lyases may be introduced if the chromophore-related results motivate that extension (Hu et al., 2020). These are conditional developments rather than independent required chapters.

The cell-free reconstruction can demonstrate biochemical sufficiency under defined conditions. Conclusions about timing, localization, necessity or completeness of the pathway in intact cells require additional evidence.

# Integration and work progression

The two chapters address complementary questions about photosynthetic antennae. Chapter 1 establishes the composition, organization and spectroscopic properties of recovered assemblies. Chapter 2 examines molecular factors that alter an antenna substrate. Their integration rests on the relationship between protein–pigment organization and measurable molecular behavior; a shared regulatory mechanism is not required.

| Stage | Chapter 1 | Chapter 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Establish interpretable starting material | Select pilot material; document harvest and recovery; identify a reproducible antenna fraction | Confirm substrate and recombinant-protein batches, integrity and assay stability |
| Establish the principal observation | Resolve composition/assembly and matched spectra | Compare AmNblA, AmNblB and combined effects |
| Test an explanation | Investigate selected structural or perturbation questions | Test order, carryover, interactions and structure-informed predictions |
| Extend selectively | Additional strains or a justified light-treatment question | NblD, Clp machinery or lyases when motivated by results |
